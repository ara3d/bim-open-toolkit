Rows to Flow: six variations

The same reading in every mark, table rows on the left becoming a flow that gathers into a node or a building on the right, drawn six times under six different influences. Accent #2f66ce on white and white on accent; the wordmark is Space Grotesk 700 at 15 px. Each mark is one path with one fill in a 24 × 24 viewBox, except the isometric one, which uses two fills as allowed.

2a: Swiss grid Bauhaus / Swiss geometric

mark-2a-swiss.svg · lockup-2a-swiss.svg

Only two shapes, the rectangle and the circle, on a strict 24-unit grid with 3-unit strokes. Three row bars run into a vertical collector, the collector meets one circle. There is no curve except the node, no diagonal, and no element thinner than 3 units, so nothing is lost at any size. It is the most honest translation of the product's claim into geometry: rows, a bus, a result, and the restraint reads as engineering rather than decoration.

2b: Metro map Transit diagram

mark-2b-metro.svg · lockup-2b-metro.svg

Three lines leave three termini, run straight, bend at exactly 45 degrees and arrive at one interchange. Line weight 2.4 units, terminus dots larger than the line, a joint dot at each bend so the corner is rounded the way Beck's London map rounds it. The metro vocabulary already means "many routes, one network" to anyone who has read a transit map, and the three lines could take the three pack hues on a start page without changing the geometry.

2c: Section drawing Architectural drawing

mark-2c-drawing.svg · lockup-2c-drawing.svg

Drawn with one pen weight, 1.4 units, as an architect's section: on the left a schedule with a margin line and three rows, on the right a four-storey building in section with a roof, three slabs and a heavier ground line, and three thin leaders carrying the rows into the middle floor. It is the only variant where the right-hand element is a building rather than an abstract node, which puts BIM back in the picture, and it is the one an architect would recognise as their own drawing convention.

2d: Terminal Pixel / terminal

mark-2d-pixel.svg · lockup-2d-pixel.svg

Everything sits on a 12 × 12 grid of 2-unit cells. The rows are runs of cells, the flow is a staircase of single cells stepping one cell down and one cell right per step, the node is a solid 3 × 3 block. There are no curves and no diagonals, only cells, so the mark is identical at every integer multiple of 12 px and never antialiases at 24 or 48. It speaks to the developer and MCP side of the audience, the people who meet BimOpenFlow in a terminal first.

2e: O for Open Monogram / letterform

mark-2e-monogram.svg · lockup-2e-monogram.svg

The node is a letter: a geometric O with a stroke the same 2.6 units as the rows, so the mark is one monoline figure. Three rows leave the left edge, the outer two turn through 45 degrees, and all three stop inside the ring's band, so the O is also the hub. Read left to right it is a schedule becoming an Open result; read as a glyph it is the O of OpenFlow and could sit alone as a favicon the way a single letter does. It is the variant that most looks like a brand rather than an icon.

2f: Slabs and block Isometric / solid (two fills)

mark-2f-isometric.svg · lockup-2f-isometric.svg · fills #2f66ce and #1d479a (reversed: white and #b9cdf5)

Rows become physical: three thin slabs stacked in true 30-degree isometric, their long axis pointing at a solid cube on the right. Top faces take the accent, the two visible sides take a darker blue, so the pair of fills does the work that outlines would do. It is the one variant with depth and the one that most resembles the building blocks of the current cube mark, so it would be the smoothest handover from today's command bar; the slabs also recall storeys, which keeps the building in the metaphor without drawing one.

Weakest at: the flow. Nothing joins the slabs to the cube, so the "gathering" is implied by direction only, and the 1.2-unit slab edges vanish at 16 px, leaving three stripes beside a hexagon; it also needs two colours, so it cannot be a single path in code.

Ranking and pick

Favicon legibility (16 px)Distinctiveness
1. 2a Swiss grid: 3-unit strokes, nothing thinner1. 2e O for Open: a letterform, not an icon
2. 2d Terminal: built on the pixel grid2. 2c Section drawing: nobody else draws a schedule as a section
3. 2e O for Open: holds if the counter stays open3. 2f Slabs and block: the only one with depth
4. 2b Metro map: lines fine, termini vanish4. 2d Terminal: memorable, but memorable as a game
5. 2f Slabs and block: slab edges vanish5. 2b Metro map: shares a silhouette with git-merge
6. 2c Section drawing: sub-pixel lines6. 2a Swiss grid: reads as E plus bullet

Pick: 2e, O for Open, with 2a as the fallback. 2e is the only variant that places in the top three on both criteria: the ring is a 10-unit shape that survives 16 px, and the letter gives the mark a meaning ("Open") that none of the merge-shaped variants has, so it does not need the wordmark to be itself. 2a wins outright on legibility and is the cheapest to build and to draw by hand on a whiteboard, which is why it is the fallback if the O's counter proves to fill in on the owner's screens. 2c and 2f are the ones to keep for a start page or a paper figure at 96 px, where their detail pays off, but neither should be the favicon.
